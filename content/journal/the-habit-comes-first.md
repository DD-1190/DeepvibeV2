## One gesture, many tools

Think about the last time something small and good happened at a meal. A dish arrived looking better than expected, or someone at the table said something funny. Before the moment had quite finished, a phone came out.

What happened next depends on who was holding it. One person sends a photo to the family group. Another posts a picture with a short caption. Someone younger records eight seconds of video with a song over it. An older relative might, days later, forward the photo by email with a long note attached.

Four different tools, one gesture: *look at this, I wanted you to see it too.*

I keep coming back to that gesture when people ask what consumer AI will become. Most of the conversation is about capability: what the models can do, how fast, at what cost. I'm more curious about something quieter. Which of our existing habits could AI slip into so naturally that we stop noticing it's there? And which habits should it stay out of?

## The want stays; the wrapper changes

A little history helps here, as long as we don't tidy it up too much.

Network email began as an engineer's experiment. In 1971, [Ray Tomlinson sent messages between two computers on the ARPANET](https://www.npr.org/2016/03/06/469428062/ray-tomlinson-inventor-of-modern-email-has), a network built for the US government, and chose the @ sign to separate a person from their machine. For years email lived mostly inside universities, labs and companies.

Then it went personal. When [Hotmail launched on 4 July 1996](https://news.microsoft.com/?p=12169), the pitch was an email address that belonged to you, not to your employer or internet provider, reachable from any browser. Within three years it had more than 40 million users. People wrote long letters, forwarded jokes, and attached photos that took minutes to download.

Years later, phone-first apps made sharing pictures almost effortless. [Instagram launched on iPhone in October 2010](https://www.britannica.com/money/Instagram) and passed a million users in under three months. A decade after that, [Instagram added Reels in August 2020](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/introducing-instagram-reels-announcement): short, multi-clip videos with music and effects.

It's tempting to read that as a ladder, each rung replacing the last. That isn't what happened. Email is still everywhere, for work and for life. Photo sharing didn't kill writing, and short video didn't kill photos. These tools overlap, and many people use all of them in the same day, for different moods and different people. Email also wasn't simply a "business" tool that later "reached" consumers; personal messages ran through it from early on.

What I take from the story is narrower and, I think, more useful. The formats kept changing; the reasons people used them barely moved. We wanted to share what we saw, show how we felt, tell a small story, and feel close to people who weren't in the room.

## Why habits, not features, seem to decide

This next part is my interpretation, not a law.

The tools that settled into everyday life seem to have done three things well. They fitted a moment that already existed: the dinner, the holiday, the boring commute. They cut the effort of something people already wanted to do: fewer steps between *I saw this* and *you've seen it*. And they were socially legible: the people around you understood what you were doing when you did it.

None of these is about technical power. Early webmail was slow and limited. The first photo apps took small, square, heavily filtered pictures. They won a place anyway, because they fitted the shape of an ordinary day.

That gives me a working thesis: **technology becomes part of everyday life when it fits a human habit, and AI's consumer future may depend on doing the same.** I hold it loosely, and I'll push against it below.

## AI is already more personal than its reputation

Public conversation about AI is dominated by work: drafting, summarising, analysing, automating. That's real, but it's not the whole picture.

In 2025, OpenAI published a large study of how people use ChatGPT's consumer plans, written with Harvard economist David Deming. It found that [roughly 70% of consumer usage was not work-related](https://openai.com/index/how-people-are-using-chatgpt/), and that the largest share of messages were people asking for information or advice rather than handing off tasks. One company's data about its own product only tells part of the story, but it suggests the "AI is a business tool" frame is already incomplete.

So maybe the question isn't *when will AI arrive in personal life?* It's already there, mostly through a chat window. The more interesting question is what shape it takes when it leaves the chat window.

## Which habits could AI fit?

If we follow the habit idea, the best candidates are things people already do many times a day, often clumsily:

- **Asking someone.** "What's the word for…", "Is this safe to eat?", "How do I reply to this?" We already turn to friends, search boxes and group chats.
- **Remembering.** Where we parked, what a colleague said, the name of that restaurant.
- **Translating and explaining.** A menu abroad, a form in legal language, a medicine leaflet.
- **Describing what we see.** To ourselves, or to someone who can't see it.

This is where devices like smart glasses become an interesting test, not because they are futuristic but because they piggyback on something people already do: wear glasses. Meta's [Ray-Ban Display glasses went on sale in the US on 30 September 2025](https://www.meta.com/blog/meta-ray-ban-display-ai-glasses-connect-2025/) at $799, with a small in-lens display and a wristband that reads finger movements. Its display-free glasses sell in larger numbers: [more than 7 million Meta smart glasses were sold in 2025](https://heise.de/-11173680), according to its eyewear partner EssilorLuxottica.

Those are meaningful numbers for a young category. They are also small next to the phone in everyone's pocket. Whether glasses become a habit or remain a gadget is genuinely open.

## The tensions we shouldn't skip

Here's where my own thesis starts to wobble, and I think it should.

**Fitting a habit can mean the habit of the person nearby.** Glasses were tried before. Google's Glass Explorer edition went on sale in 2013 and was withdrawn from consumer sale in January 2015. Market research at the time found that while [43% of consumers were interested, 50% had privacy concerns](https://www.informationweek.com/mobile-devices/google-glass-killed-for-now), and some bars and restaurants banned it. The wearer could have had a perfectly good habit; the people across the table hadn't agreed to be part of it. Today's glasses show a light when they record. That's a real step, but a small light is not the same as consent.

**People want control, not just convenience.** In a June 2025 survey, [half of US adults said the growing use of AI in daily life made them more concerned than excited](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2025/09/17/how-americans-view-ai-and-its-impact-on-people-and-society/), against one in ten who felt more excited than concerned. Most said they wanted more control over how AI is used in their lives. That's a US snapshot, not the world, but it's a useful corrective to any story where adoption is inevitable.

**"Always available" can quietly become "always there".** A tool that fits a habit perfectly is easy to reach for, and that cuts both ways. Feeds and short video fit our habits so well that many of us now spend time with them we didn't plan to. An AI that is always listening, always ready, could be a helpful friend or a constant small interruption. Some people will want it close. Many will want it off by default, and only when asked.

**Social comfort moves slower than technology.** We learned, slowly, when it's rude to check a phone. We'll need the same unwritten rules for talking to an assistant in public, or wearing a camera on our face at a funeral, a classroom or a first date. Those rules aren't designed in a lab; they emerge, and they can say no.

## A sharper version of the idea

So here's where I've landed, for now.

Fitting a habit looks necessary for AI to become ordinary, but it isn't sufficient, and it isn't automatically good. The tools that earned a lasting place in our lives fitted what we already did *and* left us feeling in charge of when and how we did it. Email waits until you open it. A photo is shared when you press send.

If consumer AI follows that path, the winners may not be the most capable systems. They may be the ones that understand the moment they're entering, respect the people who never chose to be in it, and know when to stay quiet. The gesture at the dinner table, *look at this, I want you to see it too*, will still be there. The question is whether AI helps us make it, or starts making it for us.
